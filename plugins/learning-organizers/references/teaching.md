# Teaching guide

What each organizer is for, how to run it with learners, and where it fails. Adapted from
the Federal Highway Administration's National Highway Institute *LearnersFirst* instructor
handbook (fhwaapps.fhwa.dot.gov/nhit/LearnersFirst), a U.S. federal government
publication. Its examples come from NHI Instructor Development Course participants, cited
in each worked example's `source`.

**Common to all.** A graphic organizer is a one-page form that shows the relationships
between facts, terms or ideas. It scaffolds learning: learners sort a lot of information,
see patterns and compare perspectives. If the task stops at filling in boxes, they may not
build the understanding you intended. Follow it with an application step, such as a
discussion, a written explanation or a plan for action.

Delivery formats in the handbook: instructor-led (print the learner PDF), web-based (an
online form or discussion post) and web conference (polls, chat, breakout rooms). Each
section below names the format adjustment where the handbook gives one.

## T-chart
- **For:** two facets of one topic (pros/cons, advantages/disadvantages, before/after), for
  brainstorming, decisions and pre-writing. Fast to make, fits any topic.
- **Fails:** only two sides. With a third option, use an attribute matrix.

## Attribute matrix (decision matrix)
- **For:** comparing several options on shared criteria, to prioritise, decide, solve a
  problem or defend a decision already made. Weight the criteria and total each option's
  scores: the highest total is the first choice.
- **Fails:** best with quantifiable criteria. It becomes unwieldy with too many options.
  Keep about 2–5.

## Flow chart
- **For:** the sequential steps of a process: actions, inputs and outputs, decision points,
  people, time and measures. Use it to understand, explain, improve or plan a process.
  Give one to learners, or have them draw their own to show their understanding.
- **Fails:** following a path takes manual tracing, and big charts get hard to read.
  Split them by phase.

## Mind map
- **For:** brainstorming, meeting notes, outlining a report, listing tasks. The main idea
  sits in the centre, with related ideas radiating out.
- **Rules:** key words and short phrases, not sentences. Best for less complex ideas.

## Concept map
- **For:** the "big picture" of a course and how its topics fit together, or an assessment
  of a learner's understanding. Concepts are arranged hierarchically and joined by lines
  labelled with **linking phrases**. The label is what separates it from a mind map.
- **Run it:** write a focus question from a learning outcome. Learners list concepts, order
  them from general to specific, draft the map, and add to it during the lesson. Allow
  several connections per concept. As practice, have partners compare maps.
- **Fails:** as an assessment it needs a rubric or checklist, and grading takes time. In
  instructor-led sessions, tables can arrange printed concept cards on easel paper. In a
  web conference, show the course map at the start of each lesson.

## K-W-L chart
- **For:** activating prior knowledge (**K**now) and setting learning intentions (**W**ant
  to know, written as questions), then recording what was **L**earned in the learners'
  own words. A fourth column, **H**ow I will find out, is a common extension.
- **Run it:** brainstorm K, write W as questions and have a few volunteers share them. At
  the end, review L along with "aha" moments and unanswered questions. Give resources for
  the questions still open.
- **Fails:** learners can record misconceptions in K and hold on to them. Return to K at
  the end.

## Anticipation guide
- **For:** linking new information to prior beliefs, and giving learners a purpose for
  listening: to confirm or rethink their first view.
- **Good statements** (2–10 of them): focus on the learning outcomes, rest on course
  material that supports or opposes them, challenge beliefs, and stay general rather than
  specific.
- **Run it:** learners mark each statement alone, discuss it with a partner, and may
  change their mark. During the lesson they note where they found evidence. At the end,
  ask who changed position, and ask for specific examples. Online, use poll questions at
  the start and again at the end.

## Action plan
- **For:** turning a goal into actionable tasks on a timeline. Each task has an owner and a
  deadline, and the plan can also list resources and measures of progress. It is a living
  document.
- **Run it:** at the end of a course, learners plan two or three changes they will make at
  work. They list the resources, the incremental steps, the people who can help and their
  own deadlines.

## Structured notes
- **For:** prompting recall and processing at key points in a lesson. They keep learners
  engaged and become a take-away. Four formats: a question with (numbered) lines; a copy of
  a slide or screenshot with blanks; a blank or partly completed diagram; open group-notes
  space with directions.
- **Fails:** note-taking that becomes predictable is boring. Vary the formats. When you
  reveal answers on a slide, walk around, because writing takes longer than clicking.

## Assessment checklist
- **For:** stating the skills, behaviours or attitudes learners must show, so that
  learners, peers and instructors can all monitor them. Answers are usually Yes/No.
- **Write it:** take the key skills and conditions (time limits, resources) from a
  learning outcome. Write a clear, specific, **observable** description of each as a
  statement or question. Add a date line and space for comments. Share it with learners at
  the start of training.

## Rubric
- **For:** scoring or giving feedback on performances that a multiple-choice test cannot
  measure: papers, projects, presentations, demonstrations. Each element gets an explicit
  description at every quality level, and important elements can carry extra weight.
- **Fails:** rubrics take time to write, explain and apply. With several graders, train
  them to score consistently.

## Self-questioning
- **For:** deeper engagement. Learners generate and answer high-level questions about a
  text or lecture before, during or after it.
- **Stems:** before ("Why am I reading this?", "How can I use it?"); during ("What is the
  main idea of …?", "How does this relate to what I know?", "What conflicts with my
  experience?"); after ("How has my knowledge changed?", "What questions remain?", "How
  will I apply this on the job?").
- **Fails:** it takes time and adds mental load. Keep it to a few questions.

## Summaries and tickets (structured-notes recipes)
- **3-2-1 summary:** three main points, two ideas the learner disagrees with or finds
  controversial, and one question. Use a `lines` section for each.
- **Entrance and exit tickets (quick writes):** one open prompt, 2–10 minutes, unsigned,
  not graded for spelling. Use a `lines` or `group` section.
